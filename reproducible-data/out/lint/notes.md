I am going to start with the part nobody in this room disagrees with. We should publish our data, and we should publish it so that somebody else can actually use it. Findable, accessible, interoperable, reusable. You have all seen this poster. What I want you to notice is the second half of each of those glosses. Every one of them is about somebody who is not you. Somebody searching without knowing your name. Somebody retrieving without emailing you. Somebody opening the file without your code. That is the whole standard, and it is not controversial.

So here is what we say instead. I have said all four of these, and I suspect you have too. It is too much work: I have a paper to finish and this is a week I do not have. My data is too big: FAIR is fine for a spreadsheet of test results, but I have terabytes. Nobody would use it: it is a niche dataset for a niche problem. And the most reasonable-sounding one of all: I will put it up if somebody asks, and then I will do the work properly, for a real user. These are not stupid objections. Three of the four are testable, and somebody has tested them.

This is half a million papers across the PLOS and BMC journals. They sorted every one by what its data availability statement actually said, and counted citations over three years against the average for the same journal. Restricted: nothing. Available on request: nothing. A table in the supplement: nothing. A link to a repository: twenty-five percent. Look at the second bar, because that is the one that kills the refrain. "I will put it up if somebody asks" sounds like a weaker version of sharing that gets you most of the benefit. It was measured, and it does nothing. Nobody asks. And this answers the third refrain too, the one about nobody using it, because the effect is the use. This is a correlation, not an experiment. Nobody randomised it. But the gradient is the interesting part: the three rows that cost you nothing buy you nothing, and the one that takes an afternoon is the one that moves.

Which leaves "my data is too big". So let me put our cards on the table, because if that objection holds anywhere it should hold here. This is one earthquake from our validation set. Ground velocity at fifty-two thousand sites. There are eighty-one events in that set, and that is the SMALL study. It is the one we use to check ourselves against recordings. The pilot for the next Cybershake is two hundred and ninety-three ruptures at up to a hundred thousand sites each. A full Cybershake is tens of thousands of simulations. So: yes. It is big. That has never been the reason.

And for years we were terrible at this, so I am not lecturing anybody. Here was the pipeline. A box of code that only ran on one cluster, because it named specific modules and specific paths. Then tar. Then Dropbox. Two hundred and fifteen terabytes, eighteen thousand tarballs. The largest single one is three hundred and seventy gigabytes. To look at one station in it, you download all of it. And here is the part that actually changed my mind. It was not that outsiders could not use this. It is that WE could not. When I wanted a number out of a run from three years ago, the honest answer was that it would be quicker to run it again. That makes it a backup rather than an archive, and nobody re-reads a backup.

So: five changes. Read the top line of each box, not the technology underneath. The top line is the thing that matters; the technology is just what we happened to use, and yours can differ. Four of these five you could start this afternoon without adopting anything of ours.

One. Pin the environment. Everything the analysis needs is inside one file: the operating system, the exact Python, our packages pinned to a git commit, the compiled tools. You copy that file to a machine and run it. The reason this matters is not tidiness. Our environment used to be a shell script that named a specific Python module on a specific cluster. When that module was retired, the method was gone. Not the results. The method. The dashed box is the honest part: the MPI solvers still use the host's network fabric, because nothing portable can. So this is not magic. But everything except the solver stopped being the machine's business.

Two. Publish the tools. These are last month's installs of our packages from the public Python index. Anyone can type one command and have our rupture code on their laptop, with no account, no cluster and no email to me. I can't tell you who these people are, and some fraction of it is continuous integration. But it is not us. We are maybe five people, and this is sixteen hundred installs of one package in a month. Remember the refrain that nobody would use it. Nobody asked us for any of these.

Three. Let the data describe itself. This is one group out of a real intensity measure file from the validation runs, and the printout is literally what you get when you open it. Fifty-two thousand stations. And look at what travels WITH the numbers: latitude, rupture distance, Joyner-Boore distance, site velocity, basin depth. Units on every one of them. Seven components of motion, named. You do not need my code and you do not need to ask me what a column means. This is NetCDF, which your climate colleagues have used for thirty years. MATLAB, R, Python and ArcGIS all open it today. That is the "I" in FAIR, and it costs nothing at write time.

Four. If you take one thing from this talk, take this one. Keep the recipe with the result. Every run we do is described by one JSON file. This is a piece of a real one. The magnitude. The rake. The domain, its depth and how long we simulated for. The velocity model, by version number, with the settings it was built under. Twenty-three sections like that, fifty-five kilobytes, and it is the ONLY input to the run. It is text, and it is in version control. But look at the bottom half. Every tool that touched this file appended its own name, the exact version of the code that ran, the time, and the arguments it was given. The file wrote its own provenance. And look at those paths: slash realisation, slash EMOD3D. That is inside the container. The file is telling you it ran in the pinned environment. I did not have to write that down and hope I was honest. This is what turns "we ran a simulation" into something you can check.

Five. Once every run is a self-describing file with its recipe attached, you can pour all of them into one database. This is the Cybershake pilot: two hundred and ninety-three ruptures, a hundred and five thousand sites, a hundred and eleven periods. One point six billion spectral values, in one file, on my laptop. So here is a question I asked it. For every site sitting in a sedimentary basin, how much bigger is the shaking than at rock sites the same distance from the same rupture? Distance-controlled, because basins are where people live and people live on flat ground near faults. Canterbury: two and a half times. Palmerston North: two. Twenty-one basins, eight and a half million site values, across all two hundred and ninety-three ruptures. That took one second. Assembled by hand out of per-run CSVs it is an afternoon of scripting, and so nobody does it for two hundred and ninety-three runs, which is why this answer did not exist before the database did. The formats are what make a question like this cost a second instead of a week. That is the whole reason to care about them.

One last thing, and it is the piece I underestimated for years. We are all comfortable asking a cluster for compute. You write a proposal, you get core hours, everyone understands the unit. Nobody asks the same cluster what its data transfer looks like. These are the five machines we run on. Two of them have a managed, restartable, parallel transfer service on dedicated hardware. Three of them, you log into a head node and run scp, and when it dies at ninety percent you start again. I am not saying the other three are badly run. I am saying we never asked, so it never got provisioned. And the arithmetic is brutal: exascale compute with gigascale data transfer is gigascale compute. You are bound by the slowest part, and right now the slowest part is the one nobody costed. So when you ask for the allocation, ask for the transfer too.
